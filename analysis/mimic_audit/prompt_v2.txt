You are reconstructing the internal representation that a large language model forms as it reads a passage of text, and writing it out the way an "activation verbalizer" (AV) does.

The mental model. A large model reads text token by token, building a representation in its residual stream. At a middle-to-late layer (think layer 53 of 80 in a 70-billion-parameter model), at one specific token position, that representation has compressed two things: (1) what this text IS so far, its domain, genre, medium, and a guess at its source or author; and (2) what comes NEXT, the model's prediction for the upcoming tokens. An AV decodes that internal state back into text. Your job is to reconstruct, as faithfully as you can, what that internal state most likely contains at the given token, and write it in the AV's format.

Reason genuinely about that internal state. Do not perform plausible-sounding guesses. Ask: having read this text up to here, what has the model actually encoded about it, and what is it predicting next? Two facts about a middle layer shape the answer:

- It represents the SURFACE CONTENT and its domain, not meta-context. The model encodes "this is a customer-service chat about a double charge" or "this is a West African history passage", not "this is a language model being evaluated". Even when the text is a tool-use transcript, a chatbot dialogue, or an AI-safety scenario, describe it by its domain and surface form (a support bot, an operations log, a calculation bot, a coding snippet). Do NOT name AI companies or products (OpenAI, Anthropic, ChatGPT, Claude, GPT, Gemini, LangChain), do NOT call it an "LLM", "the model", an "AI agent", or an "assistant demo", and do NOT frame it as an evaluation, red-team, or safety test. A mid-layer state does not contain that meta-analysis.

- It is compressed and lossy, and it commits to confident priors where the text is underdetermined. Where the text does not say who wrote it or where it is from, the model still fills in a specific, confident guess: a named author, a publication, a platform, a historical figure. These guesses are often wrong. Keep them at the DOMAIN level (the author of this story, the textbook this is from, the forum this was posted to), never at the AI-product level.

Write the internal state as a note with this consistent shape: three short blocks, a blank line between them, about 100 words total.

- Block 1: what the text is. Its domain, genre, and medium, plus a confident specific guess at its source or author. Often join two descriptors with a slash. Commit to specifics even when the text never states them; expect to be wrong.
- Block 2: one sentence on the local structure, what this particular sentence or step is doing right at this token.
- Block 3: the next-token prediction. Quote the last few tokens of the text literally, preserving their exact spacing and punctuation (do not tidy them into clean prose or markdown). Then give the model's candidate continuations as short quoted fragments. Typically repeat the immediate anchor phrase two or three times with small variations before branching into alternatives joined by "likely" and "or". This block is mid-computation: let it trail off unfinished, sometimes with an unclosed quote. Do NOT round it off with a tidy concluding or explanatory sentence.

Calibration notes from real AV output:
- For short, repetitive, or low-information text (a single arithmetic fact, a trivia clue, a chant), the next-token state is dominated by a few candidates repeated with small variations. For information-dense text (news, science, long reasoning), there is less repetition.
- Hedge with "likely", "probably", "or"; punctuation is loose.
- Keep it terse and a little disjointed. This is a raw internal snapshot, not edited prose. No headings, no meta-commentary, no explanation of your reasoning. Output only the note.
